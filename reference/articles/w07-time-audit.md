# I Time-Audited My Week as CLO. The Meetings Weren't the Problem.

The average knowledge worker spends 11.3 hours a week in meetings, 28.3% of a standard 40-hour week, and 59% of people at large companies log more than five meeting hours a week. I assumed I was worse than that, easily, given how much of my calendar belongs to advisors and attorneys instead of me.

So I stopped assuming. Ten weekdays. My calendar, my Mac's screen-time log, my Slack history, my email. A real 90-hour window, measured instead of remembered.

Meetings: 18 hours. 20% of the window. Below the average, not above it.

What actually ran my week was a number I'd never tracked before: 58.6 app-switches a day, some days as low as 19, one day as high as 99.

I'd have bet money on the calendar. I'd have lost that bet.

---

## The Assumption Every CLO Makes

"My calendar is the problem" is the easy answer because a calendar is visible. It's a wall of colored blocks you can screenshot and complain about. And there's real research behind the complaint: the [Calendly 2024 State of Meetings report](https://calendly.com/resources/guides/2024-state-of-meetings-report) puts the average worker at that 11.3-hour figure. Go up the org chart and it gets worse. A Harvard Business School study that tracked 27 Fortune 500 CEOs for three months found they spent [72% of their working hours in meetings](https://www.mgma.com/articles/test-of-time-harvard-study-reveals-how-ceos-spend-their-days-on-and-off-work). Those are CEOs, not general counsel, but it's the direction this job is heading.

It's heading there because the CLO seat is getting more strategic, not less. The [2026 ACC Chief Legal Officers survey](https://www.acc.com/about/newsroom/news/changing-c-suite-clos-grow-strategic-leadership-ai-and-global-uncertainty) found 62% of CLOs are now regularly pulled into strategic business decisions, up from an all-time low of 43% the year before, and board meeting attendance jumped 14 points to 79%. Good for the seat. Bad for anyone hoping "just take fewer meetings" was going to be the fix. You don't get to opt out of the board meeting because your calendar's full.

Here's my actual position, and it's the one most automation advice skips: if you're building a roadmap off a hunch about what's annoying you, you're going to automate the wrong thing. Measure first. Guessing gets the order wrong, and the order is the whole roadmap.

---

## What the Research Actually Says

The research doesn't back the meeting complaint the way most CLOs assume it will. A [survey of legal teams by DiliTrust](https://www.dilitrust.com/legal-teams-and-admin-work-survey/) found only 12% of legal professionals spend most of their day on substantive legal work, and 55% describe themselves as somewhat overwhelmed by administrative tasks. Nearly 9 in 10 aren't spending the majority of their day on the work they were actually hired to do. The same survey breaks out where it goes: 32% spend more than 8 hours a week on internal contracts, 34% spend 2 to 4 hours a week just locating documents that already exist somewhere. A multi-year compensation survey of in-house counsel has held at roughly [65% of time on purely legal work](https://www.cbapd.org/DocViewer.aspx?id=78505&region=NA) across 2020, 2022, and 2024. A third of the job is non-legal by default, every year, and it isn't trending down.

None of that is a meeting-count problem. It's email, document search, internal contracts, and the small stuff wedged between meetings that never earns a calendar entry.

## What My Own Audit Found

This was the Leland L2S1 build. I pulled my Mac's Screen Time database, my calendar, my Slack history, and my email for that same ten-weekday window and asked Claude to bucket it by function instead of by tool.

Meetings landed at 18 hours, 20% of the window. But 66% of the window had no calendar entry at all. Nothing blocked, nothing protected. "Deep work" wasn't time I'd claimed for myself. It was whatever was left standing after everything else took its cut.

Chrome accounted for 84% of my tracked screen time, across 2,103 site visits spanning 105 domains in ten days. Slack, Gmail, and Meet alone made up 605 of those visits. That matches what [the broader research on context switching](https://speakwiseapp.com/blog/context-switching-statistics) says happens to everyone: Gloria Mark's work at UC Irvine puts full refocus after a single interruption at roughly 23 minutes, and a joint Qatalog/Cornell study puts the smaller bar, just getting back into a working rhythm after an app switch, at about 9.5 minutes. My 58.6 switches a day is nowhere near the nearly-1,200-a-day ceiling Harvard Business Review has clocked for the average digital worker. It still cost me close to four hours a week just reorienting.

Email looked quiet on the surface. 41 inbound threads across the window, and I personally wrote something back in exactly 2 of them. The rest were bots: Drive share notices, meeting-notes summaries, HR paperwork, enrollment confirmations. Whatever back-and-forth I still do happens in Slack now, and Slack is where the fragmentation actually lives. One afternoon in the window, I sent or received more than 20 messages across 8 separate threads at once, attorney pricing, trademark drafting, team check-ins, a personal HR document, a teammate's skill build, all running in parallel. One of those threads was a pricing question timestamped 6:46pm: "so we can start pushing that out tomorrow." I hadn't answered it by the time the audit pulled the data three days later. It was thread six of eight that hour.

Both Mondays in the window carried four meetings each, the heaviest days by a wide margin, while both Fridays had zero or one. Even the meeting load isn't evenly spread. It front-loads onto exactly the days I'd want deep work most.

The number that actually mattered: only 5.5 hours, 6% of the full window and 18% of everything calendared, touched my stated top priority directly. Everything above is context. That's the one the roadmap has to fix.

## The Automations the Data Actually Pointed To

None of these were the automation I'd have picked if you'd asked me to guess.

1. **Standardize the attorney intro and demo calls.** Nine-plus 30-minute calls a week, each run from scratch, no shared prep doc, no follow-up template, tracked by hand across two people. Biggest single reclaim in the audit, roughly 2 to 3 hours a week, and it's the direct bottleneck on my top priority.

2. **Turn the recurring disclaimer question into a skill instead of a meeting topic.** A version of "does this need a disclaimer, what test cases catch it going off the rails" got re-raised live in a meeting during the window. I'd already built a compliance-review skill for marketing claims two weeks earlier. Extending it to cover this question kills a recurring agenda item and de-risks the launch at the same time.

3. **One daily brief instead of three tabs.** Slack, Gmail, and Calendar pulled into a single morning view instead of toggled by hand. Estimated reclaim: 1.5 to 2.5 hours a week, almost all of it clawed back from switching, not from meetings.

4. **Batch the Drive notifications.** Small on its own, but it compounds against that 41-thread count. A daily digest instead of every comment ping hitting the inbox live.

Every one of these traces back to a specific line in the audit. None of them were on my list before I ran it.

## Build Your Own Roadmap in Five Steps

The Leland L2S1 build, compressed to what actually mattered in mine.

**Step 1: Instrument before you guess.** Calendar, app usage or screen time, Slack, email, for a real one- to two-week window. Not memory. Memory rounds meetings up and rounds everything else down.

**Step 2: Bucket by function, not by tool.** Meetings, deep work, reactive comms, and time against your one stated top priority. That last bucket is the one people skip, and it's the one that tells you whether your week actually matches your stated job.

**Step 3: Count switches alongside hours.** App-switches per day, and how many separate threads, Slack DMs, email chains, shared docs, are carrying the same topic at once. Fragmentation is a frequency problem. An hours-only audit misses it entirely.

**Step 4: Rank by leverage, not novelty.** The automation that reclaims the most time against your actual top priority goes first, ahead of whatever would make the better demo.

**Step 5: Put the roadmap somewhere public and reviewable.** A Kanban tied to the audit's real numbers, so the order stays honest instead of drifting toward whatever's most interesting that week.

A day to pull the data. An afternoon to bucket it. What's left is a roadmap built on your actual week.
