## Why This Matters Now: The ABA's First AI Ethics Guidance

In July 2024, the American Bar Association issued its first ethics guidance on AI tools. It didn't create new rules. It clarified existing ones.

**Rule 1.1: Competence**

A lawyer must provide competent representation, which includes the legal knowledge, skill, preparation, and experience reasonably necessary for the representation.

Translation: If AI is now part of how your company operates, you need to understand AI well enough to know if it's creating risk.

You don't need to be an AI engineer. You need to ask three questions and understand the answers:
1. What does this AI actually do?
2. Where could it break or cause harm?
3. Can I explain it to the board?

If you can't answer all three, you don't yet have competence to govern it.

**Rule 1.3: Diligence**

A lawyer shall act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing a client. Diligence also requires attention to the consequences for the client of delayed action.

Translation: Ignoring AI isn't caution. It's negligence. Your company is moving forward with AI or without you. If you're not in the room understanding it, the risk isn't lower—it's hidden.

The founders you work for need counsel who can help them move fast. Not counsel who says no because they're uncomfortable with something they don't understand.

## How to Say Yes While Saying No to Risk

Here's the truth that most CLOs miss: Technical competence and business agility aren't at odds.

The founders I've worked with didn't want me to block them. They wanted me to help them move faster by being clear about the boundaries.

At each company, I built a compliance framework specific to what we were doing:

**The AI Governance Checklist**

Before we ship any AI feature, marketing claim, or customer-facing application:

1. Can I explain what this AI does and how it could break?
2. Does it touch regulated activity (financial transactions, personal health data, attorney-client privilege)?
3. What data does it see, and who owns that data?
4. If this fails, what's the worst case?
5. Can I defend this to a regulator, a customer, or a jury?

That's it. Five questions. Takes 2–3 days of back-and-forth.

If you can answer all five clearly, you ship. Founders get velocity. You get accountability.

If you can't answer one of them, you don't ship yet. But you can engineer your way through the gap. You're not blocking—you're unblocking with specificity.

I did this for a debit card platform serving over 100,000 customers in their first year of operation. Fast, yes. Reckless, no. The difference was understanding what risk we were actually taking.

## The Balance That Seems Impossible (But Isn't)

Here's where ethics gets interesting.

Rule 1.1 says: Be competent. Understand what you're governing.

Rule 1.3 says: Be diligent. Don't delay your client's business because you're uncomfortable.

Independent professional judgment says: Make your own call. Don't just do what the founder wants, and don't just do what the regulator fears.

These look contradictory. How do you be diligent (move fast) and competent (understand deeply) at the same time?

You do it by separating the thinking from the speed.

**The thinking part takes time.** You research. You model. You understand the landscape. You build a framework. That's weeks of work upfront, maybe months.

**The execution part is fast.** Once you understand the landscape and have a framework, you can answer questions in days. Founders know the boundaries. They can iterate within them. You spot-check their decisions, not every decision.

This is how a team that was shipping debit cards to 100,000 customers in a year—moving incredibly fast—also had zero regulatory surprise. We weren't slow. We were clear.

## Why You Can't Ignore This

You might be thinking: "I can hire an AI consultant. I can outsource this to an outside firm. I don't need to learn AI myself."

That doesn't work.

If you don't understand AI, you can't judge whether your consultant is right. You can't know if you're asking the right questions. You can't explain the framework to your board. You can't make independent judgments about what risk the company should take.

The ABA was clear on this: Competence is your responsibility. You can get help, but the duty doesn't outsource.

And there's a deeper reason: Tech founders move fast. They test boundaries. They sometimes push past where they should. Your job isn't to block them. Your job is to help them see the edge clearly so they make their own informed decision about how close to stand.

You can't do that if you don't understand AI.

## Where to Start

You don't need to become a machine learning engineer.

Start here:

1. **Understand what your company actually does with AI.** Not theoretically. Specifically. What data? What decisions? What could go wrong?

2. **Read the ABA guidance.** (American Bar Association, July 2024: "AI Guidance.") It's 10 pages. It clarifies the existing rules in a new context.

3. **Map the regulatory landscape.** What states do you operate in? What AI regulations apply? (I did this as a 50-state matrix—happy to share the framework.)

4. **Build a framework for your company.** Not a legal template. A real checklist that lets you say yes to 80% of things faster, and gives you language for the 20% that need more work.

5. **Use AI to do this work faster.** I used Claude Code to analyze 50 states' AI legislation in a day. A lawyer reading each state individually would take weeks. Competence isn't about effort—it's about understanding. Use tools to move fast.

This isn't optional. It's not a competitive advantage—yet. Soon it will be table stakes.

CLOs who ignore AI aren't being prudent. They're choosing a liability blind spot and hoping it doesn't matter. It will.
